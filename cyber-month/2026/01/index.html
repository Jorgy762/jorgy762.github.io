[02] // Incident // Canada, Oct 2025

They turned the dials.

Hacktivists reached control systems at Canadian sites straight from the internet. The Cyber Centre and the RCMP got multiple reports.

Water

Pressure tampered with. Service to a community degraded.

Oil and gas

Tank gauge manipulated. False alarms triggered.

Farm

Grain silo temperature and humidity changed. Potentially unsafe conditions.

No target list needed. Being reachable was enough.

How it happened

These were industrial control systems (ICS). A PLC is the small industrial computer that opens a valve or runs a pump. An HMI is the screen an operator uses to watch and change it. Both are built to sit on a closed plant network. When one answers to the open internet, anyone who finds it can try to get in.

The Cyber Centre says organizations may not be chosen targets at all. They become victims of opportunity because their devices are exposed. Its fixes:

  • Inventory every internet-accessible ICS device and ask whether it needs to be reachable.
  • Replace direct exposure with a VPN that requires multi-factor authentication.
  • Apply the Cyber Centre's Cyber Security Readiness Goals.
At work

Know what at your site answers to the internet: vendor remote access, cameras, building and plant systems. If you can't answer that, find the person who can.

At home

Your router, doorbell camera and baby monitor are the home version of an exposed PLC. Change default passwords, keep firmware updated, and turn off remote access you don't use.

Sources: Cyber Centre Alert AL25-016 (29 Oct 2025); The Canadian Press; Security Affairs and BleepingComputer summaries of the alert's recommendations. The alert named no attacker.

Two tricks behind the numbers

Caller ID spoofing. The name and number on your screen are set by whoever places the call. They prove nothing. The CAFC has warned that fraudsters spoof police phone numbers and fake CAFC letterhead to sound official.

Business email compromise. A criminal gets into, or imitates, a real email account (a supplier, a boss, a lawyer) and sends one message changing where the money goes. The CAFC tracks it under spear phishing: $67.9M lost in 2025, second only to investment fraud.

At work

Any change to payment or banking details gets confirmed by phone, on a number you already had, before money moves. No exceptions for urgency or rank.

At home

"Police," "your bank" or "CRA" calling? Hang up. Call back on a number you look up yourself. Same rule for a contractor or a house deposit that suddenly needs a new account.

[07] // Pattern of life

Your watch is reporting your position.

A fitness app doesn't need to be hacked to give you away. It publishes four things, and together they build a pattern of life.

Where

The GPS trace of every route.

When

The timestamp on every activity.

Who

A public profile, often under your real name.

Routine

Repeated start points. That's your front door, your unit lines, your shift.

Combine them and anyone can see where you sleep, when you leave and when you're gone.

Open each case file.

2018The global heatmapBases light up in the dark

Strava published a heatmap built from users' activities. Analysts quickly pointed out that military bases and patrol routes in places like Afghanistan and Syria stood out clearly, because few locals used the app.

Aggregate data still shows where you are.

2024StravaLeaks: the bodyguardsClose protection, exposed

Le Monde found Strava users among the protection details of world leaders: 26 US agents, 12 members of France's presidential security group and six officers of Russia's FSO.

One Secret Service agent's profile revealed the San Francisco hotel where President Biden later stayed for his 2023 talks with Xi Jinping. Macron's bodyguards' runs placed him at a private weekend in Normandy.

The Secret Service told Le Monde its staff can't use personal devices on protective duty, but it doesn't ban off-duty social media.

Your off-duty run can locate the person you protect.

2025SubmarinesFrench Navy, January

Le Monde reported that crews of several French Navy nuclear-powered submarines had logged movements on the app while their boats were underway.

The quietest platforms leak through the loudest habits.

2026The carrier strike groupCharles de Gaulle, March

On 13 March, during the Iran war deployment, a French officer logged a seven-kilometre, 35-minute run on the deck of the Charles de Gaulle. His profile was public. Le Monde matched it to satellite imagery taken about an hour later, which showed the carrier roughly six kilometres from the run, northwest of Cyprus.

His earlier runs had already traced the group's movements: off the Cotentin coast in February, then Copenhagen, then the eastern Mediterranean. France said it was taking appropriate measures.

One public profile tracked a carrier group across a continent.

2026Again, in AugustUS bases during the Iran war

Sky News found Strava data once again exposing US bases and troops in the Middle East, eight years after the heatmap. Strava's response: it offers privacy controls and expects people in sensitive professions to use them.

The app won't protect you. Your settings will.

Home addresses too: in 2023, NC State researchers showed they could identify some users' home addresses from Strava's "anonymous" heatmap.

Lock it down: Strava

Tick each one as you do it. Garmin, Apple, Google and others have similar settings.

Locked down: 0 / 5

At work

On duty, on exercise, on a tasking or near any site: tracking off, or don't wear it. Follow your chain of command's direction on devices. If you see a colleague's public route through the lines, tell them quietly.

At home

Check your family's apps too. A spouse's or teenager's public run that starts at your door gives away the same address.

[08] // Identity

Your MFA can be walked through.

MFA blocks most account takeovers. But one technique, adversary-in-the-middle phishing, walks straight through the common kinds. The Cyber Centre detected over 100 of these campaigns against Microsoft cloud accounts between 2023 and early 2025.

The bait

A link to a login page that looks exactly like yours.

The relay

The fake page passes your password and MFA code to the real site, live.

The token

The real site issues a session token, the "stay signed in" pass. The attacker catches it.

The takeover

With the token, they're in. No password or code needed again.

The MFA ladder. Tap a rung.

At work

An MFA prompt you didn't start means someone already has your password. Deny it, then report it right away. Attackers sometimes send prompt after prompt hoping you'll tap yes to make it stop.

At home

Turn on passkeys for your email and bank where they're offered. If not, use an authenticator app over text messages.

Sources: Cyber Centre ITSM.30.031, phishing-resistant MFA (2025); Cyber Centre ITSAP.30.030; GC Guideline on MFA.

[11] // Your role

Protect. Notice. Report.

Protect

Your DND and personal accounts, your phone and watch, and what you post about training, taskings and your section.

Notice

Strangers or "recruiters" asking about your unit. Pressure to act fast or keep quiet. Prompts you didn't start. Stories built to provoke.

Report

Military: chain of command, USS / UISSO. Personal fraud: police and the CAFC at 1-888-495-8501. Scam texts: forward to 7726.

What to capture when you report

  • A screenshot showing the sender's full address or number, and the time.
  • What you did: clicked, typed a password, approved a prompt, nothing at all.
  • If you entered a password, change it from a different device and say so in your report.
  • Don't forward the suspicious message to coworkers to ask "is this real?" Use your organization's reporting method instead.

You observe and report. Credentialed MP or police investigate. Report fast, even if you clicked, even if you're not sure.
