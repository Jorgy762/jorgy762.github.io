[02] // Case // Baltic Sea, 2024 to 2026

The cable and the court.

On Christmas Day 2024, the Estlink 2 power cable between Finland and Estonia was damaged, along with four telecom cables. Between them they carry electricity and internet traffic for ordinary households and businesses. Finland suspected a tanker that had sailed from a Russian port. Twenty months later, in August 2026, an appeal court was still deciding where the crew can be tried. Canada leads NATO's brigade in Latvia, in the same region, so this story isn't far from home.

25 Dec 24
THE CUT

Estlink 2 and four telecom cables are damaged. Finnish police seize the tanker Eagle S.

14 Jan 25
NATO ACTS

Eight Baltic states and NATO's Secretary General launch Baltic Sentry to watch undersea infrastructure.

Early 25
THE DOUBT

The Washington Post reports intelligence sources pointing to crew error, not sabotage, for Baltic cable damage.

Aug 25
THE CHARGES

Prosecutors charge the captain and two officers with negligence-based offences. No one is alleged to have done it on purpose.

3 Oct 25
DISMISSED

A Helsinki court rules Finnish criminal law can't apply. The damage was in international waters.

27 Aug 26
REVIVED

The Court of Appeal disagrees: the consequences were felt in Finland. The defendants deny wrongdoing.

Swipe the timeline sideways.

Whatever the truth turns out to be, the cut was real, the intent is unproven, and the legal system is still catching up. That gap is the gray zone.

At work

Don't wait for "who did this?" before you act. Protect what you can, report what you saw, and let specialists decide what it was.

In uniform

Same rule. Report what you saw through your chain of command, and leave the "who" to the people whose job it is.

At home

When a "who did this?" story spreads fast online, wait for confirmed facts before you share it.

Sources: RFE/RL; Subtel Forum; Novaya Gazeta Europe (citing the Washington Post); Lloyd's List; Xinhua; Sweden Herald, citing Yle; The Northern Voices (appeal date); DND on the Canada-led brigade in Latvia. The case is ongoing and may be appealed again. Allegations are not findings.

Three things. Remember them.

01

It's aimed at everyone

Gray-zone activity targets the systems, signals and information that towns, workplaces and bases all run on.

[04] // What counts

A campaign that never declares itself.

Each piece can be explained away: a ship's anchor, a signal glitch, a job ad, an angry post. Together they add up to pressure on the things a country runs on, and on the people who run them. Two official views of the same thing:

Seen from Ottawa (CSIS)

Who gets targeted

CSIS says foreign states target institutions at every level of government, private companies and associations, universities, civil society groups, and ethnic, religious and cultural communities. It describes Russia's combined espionage, sabotage and influence activity as hybrid warfare, and says Canada is considered a legitimate target.

Seen from NATO

Below the threshold

Peace, crisis and conflict used to be three separate phases. Now cyberattacks hit nations below the threshold of a military attack, and social media campaigns try to destabilize communities "without a single soldier crossing a single border."

At work

Know what your organization depends on that it doesn't control: a vendor, a cable, a signal, a supplier.

In uniform

Know your reporting route before you need it: your chain of command and CFNCIU (Brief 04).

At home

The same question for the household: power, internet, phone, water, fuel. What if one stops for a day?

Sources: CSIS Public Report 2025; NATO Review, Enlarging NATO's toolbox to counter hybrid threats.

[06] // Gray zone or glitch?

Most days, it's a glitch.

Six invented headlines. Call each one. The skill isn't suspecting everything. It's noticing patterns, and knowing when you can't tell yet.

A cargo ship drags its anchor and cuts two undersea cables.

Can't tell yet. Anchors do snag cables by accident. A 2023 Finland-Estonia pipeline was damaged by a cargo ship's anchor, and the Eagle S case is still in court after twenty months. Wait for the evidence.

Airliners over a border region lose GPS for half an hour, most days, for years.

Possibly hostile. Latvia's regulator logged 820 cases in 2024 against 26 in 2022. Baltic states call it hybrid warfare, and Russia says it's defensive against drones. Either way, it's deliberate interference that endangers civilians.

A post says your town's water isn't safe to drink. It's spreading fast, pushed by dozens of brand-new accounts. The utility says the water is fine.

Possibly hostile. The pattern matters more than the claim: new accounts, coordinated spread, and an official denial. CSIS says Russian state-linked actors hide behind proxy networks that amplify messaging. A rumour about a town's water doesn't have to come from them to be worth reporting. Check the utility's own channel before you share.

A job ad on a mainstream site offers great pay to "consult" on how your industry works, from a company with no history.

Possibly hostile. CSIS says PRC intelligence services post ads through cover companies on mainstream job sites to reach Canadians with access to proprietary or classified information. Verify the company independently, and report it before you send a resume.

A friendly new contact keeps asking when your unit trains and who's in your section.

Possibly hostile. DND tells members to report suspicious messages and contact attempts to CFNCIU, and to tell their chain of command (Brief 04).

Your phone's map is off by fifty metres for a minute in a city centre.

Ordinary. Tall buildings and weak signals do this every day. One glitch isn't a plot. Pattern, scale and duration are what make jamming worth reporting.

Score: 0 / 6

At work

Report patterns, not hunches: what you saw, when, where, and how often. Specialists connect the dots.

In uniform

DND says don't engage with hostile or provocative posts. Stay quiet in public, and put the detail in your report.

At home

Before you react to something outrageous, ask who benefits if you share it.

Sources: RFE/RL; AP via The Himalayan Times; CSIS Public Report 2025; DND notice (May 2026). Headlines are invented for training.

[07] // Same systems, same stakes

The base and the town share a grid.

CSIS calls all ten of Canada's critical infrastructure sectors high-value targets for interference, espionage and sabotage. NATO makes the same point from the military side: its forces, especially those deployed in a crisis, depend heavily on the civilian and commercial sectors for transport, communications, energy, and even food and water.

Canada's ten critical infrastructure sectors (CSIS)

01Finance
02Energy and utilities
03Food
04Transportation
05Government
06Information and communications technology
07Health

10Manufacturing

NATO's baseline requirements for resilience (2016 set)

01Continuity of government and critical services
02Resilient energy supplies
03Dealing with uncontrolled movement of people
04Resilient food and water resources
05Dealing with mass casualties
06Resilient civil communications
07Resilient transport systems

It runs both ways

The two lists describe the same grid, phone network, water main and road. NATO notes the areas are connected: if one is hit, another may suffer. So a cut cable, a jammed signal or a hacked utility hurts the town, the hospital and the base at once. And the people who keep those systems up, in uniform or not, are part of the same defence.

At work

If you work in power, water, telecom, transport or supply, you're on the list above. Brief 03 covers what that means for access and reporting.

In uniform

You rely on the same systems as the town next door. Know your own fallback for communications, navigation and supply.

At home

Government of Canada guidance is to be ready to look after your household for at least 72 hours.

Sources: CSIS Public Report 2025; NATO, Resilience, civil preparedness and Article 3; NATO, Seven Baseline Requirements (2026 revision); Get Prepared. The NATO list shown is the original 2016 set. NATO published a revision in 2026.

[08] // Canada

In CSIS's own words.

From CSIS's 2025 public report, published in 2026. Paraphrased, and not predictions.

Infrastructure

All ten sectors

CSIS calls all ten critical infrastructure sectors high-value targets for interference, espionage and sabotage, including intentional service disruption.

Cyber

Hard to tell apart

Salt Typhoon is believed to have targeted Canadian telecoms. Russia-aligned groups have occasionally disrupted critical infrastructure in NATO countries, and state and non-state activity is increasingly hard to separate.

Information

Proxies and AI

Russian state-linked actors use proxy networks and AI to amplify messaging and exploit divisive topics, to erode support for Ukraine.

People

Job ads as bait

PRC intelligence services post jobs through cover companies to reach Canadians with access to proprietary or classified information. CSIS and partners also disrupted recruitment of current and former military personnel to train PRC aviators.

Sabotage

Attempts continued

CSIS says espionage and attempted sabotage continued in 2025, and that it works with allies to disrupt Russian agents.

Russia

Hybrid warfare

A combination of espionage, sabotage and influence activity, accepting the risk of collateral damage, as a response to Western support for Ukraine.

At work

An odd approach about your job, your access or your skills is worth reporting to your security team.

In uniform

Report suspicious contact to your chain of command and CFNCIU. Brief 04 has the routes.

At home

Treat unexpected, too-good job offers the way you'd treat a call from "the bank": verify first, on your own channel.

Sources: CSIS Public Report 2025, Operations and analysis. Briefs 03 and 04 carry the Salt Typhoon and reporting sources.

[09] // Everyone has a part

Who does what.

No single agency can answer something built to be ambiguous. NATO says responding to hybrid threats takes a whole-of-government approach, and that resilience is largely a national responsibility. Tap each part.

At work

Your part is the one closest to you: your systems, your dependencies, your reporting route.

In uniform

Your part includes your own conduct online. DND asks members to keep their online activity professional and to protect sensitive information.

At home

Resilience at home is dull and it works: a written plan, a corded phone, a battery radio, and someone to call.

Sources: NATO Review; Get Prepared; Cyber Centre, report a cyber incident; CSIS, reporting national security information; RFE/RL; DND notice (May 2026).
