[02] // Case // 2022

Two companies. One text message.

In 2022, employees at Twilio and Cloudflare got near-identical texts posing as their own IT teams, linking to a fake company login page. Same campaign, around the same time.

Twilio

Staff were phished. Twilio said the attackers gained unauthorized access to information related to a limited number of its customer accounts.

Cloudflare

Three employees also entered their credentials. But every employee carried a hardware security key, so the logins failed. And employees reported the texts to a security team trained for it, which blocked the phishing site and reset the exposed credentials. Cloudflare says no systems were compromised.

Two ingredients

A control that phishing can't beat (the hardware key, the top rung of the MFA ladder in Brief 01), and people who said something. Cloudflare listed a "paranoid but blame-free culture" among its own lessons. The attackers prepared for under an hour. The defenders had to match that speed.

The same pattern, nationally

In October 2025 the Cyber Centre warned that hacktivists were tampering with internet-exposed industrial control systems at Canadian sites, after it and the RCMP received multiple reports (Brief 01). Other operators could then check their own exposure. One organization's bad day became everyone else's early warning, because someone reported it.

At work

Know the one address or number to send suspicious things to. If you don't, ask today. You don't want to be hunting for it while the clock runs.

At home

Make "tell me right away" the family rule for anything strange online, and keep it free of blame, or kids and parents will hide it.

Sources: Cloudflare's own account; The Register; Cyber Centre Alert AL25-016. Cloudflare's statements are its own account of the incident.

[05] // The no-blame promise

Say the right thing.

The UK's National Cyber Security Centre has seen it in its own red-team exercises: the stigma of clicking stops people reporting, and that delays the response. Its fix is to picture the opposite, a person who isn't embarrassed to say they clicked, a security team that thanks them and gets to work on what's exposed.

Your turn

Someone on your team comes to you quietly: "I think I clicked something I shouldn't have." What do you say?

Reply B. A and C may feel like accountability, but they teach the next person to stay quiet, and the next person is the one holding the real incident. The script: thank them, ask what they did and what they entered, hand it to security. Save the lecture for later, and make it about the process, not the person.

At work

If you lead people, your first reaction to bad news is the policy. Everyone is watching what happens to the first person who reports a mistake.

At home

Same at the kitchen table. Nobody gets in trouble for telling you about a weird text. They get thanked.

Sources: Infosecurity Magazine on the NCSC blog; NCSC: Telling users to 'avoid clicking bad links' still isn't working; NCSC phishing guidance. The scenario is invented.

[06] // Report or skip?

What's worth a report.

Six situations. Report it, or skip it. Not everything needs a report, but almost everything that feels off does.

You clicked a link in a text, then realized it looked off.

Report. Especially the click. The UK NCSC says to build a culture where people report phishing, including the messages they clicked on.

You approved a sign-in prompt you didn't start.

Report, now. Someone likely has your password already. That's the MFA lesson from Brief 01.

A stranger on a professional network asks detailed questions about your unit's work.

Report. It matches the recruitment-style approaches in Brief 02. Your chain of command and unit security staff come first.

A stranger messages you with your kids' school name and says they know where you work.

Report, and save the message. Threats or criminal behaviour go to the Military Police or your local police. DND tells members to keep records of harassment and suspicious contact.

An account on your team keeps logging in at 3 a.m.

Report. It's the "different time, source and purpose" pattern from Brief 03. Let the specialists decide if it's nothing.

A newsletter you subscribed to lands a day late.

Skip. Late isn't suspicious. Look at what's being asked: a login, money, a code, or secrecy. That's what makes something worth reporting.

Score: 0 / 6

At work

When you're not sure, say so in the report: "I'm not sure this matters, but..." Specialists would rather get ten maybes than miss one real one.

At home

Report scams even when you lost nothing. A near-miss is data, and it can protect the next person.

Sources: UK NCSC, phishing guidance; Cyber Centre, report a cyber incident (individuals); DND notice (May 2026). Scenarios are invented for training.

[07] // Who gets the call

Pick your situation.

Canada has several reporting channels, and each takes different things. Tap the one that fits.

    These are the official routes as of October 2026. Numbers and forms change, so confirm before you rely on them.

    At work

    Your employer's security team comes first. The national channels are for when the incident is bigger than one workplace, or when you've been told to use them.

    At home

    Threatened or exposed? Save everything, don't reply, call the police. Money gone? Bank first. Speed matters in both.

    [09] // Two hats

    Two employers. Two reporting lines.

    As a reservist you answer to two organizations. Both want to know, and neither sees the other's half.

    Your civilian job

    Your supervisor and your employer's security team or help desk, through the official route. If you lead people, thank them first.

    The CAF

    Your chain of command and the Canadian Forces Counter-Intelligence Unit for suspicious contact (1-855-423-6248). Threats or crimes go to the Military Police or local police. Follow your own orders over anything on this page.

    When it touches both

    A recruiter-style message asking about military work arrives on your civilian work email. A CAF-related scam text hits the personal phone you also use for your job. A lost phone holds both. In each case, tell both sides. Each only sees its half of the picture, and neither can protect you from what it doesn't know.

    At work

    Tell your civilian employer what they need to protect their systems, and no more. Operational detail stays with the CAF side.

    At home

    Keep work and CAF contacts out of group chats with family, so one compromised account doesn't expose everyone.
