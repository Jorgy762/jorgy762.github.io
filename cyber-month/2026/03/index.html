[02] // Incident // Norway, April 2025

A valve opened for four hours.

At a dam in Bremanger, western Norway, someone reached the remote-control panel for one of the valves and opened it to 100%. It took the dam's operators about four hours to find and reverse it.

7 Apr
THE ACCESS

Unauthorized access to a remote-control panel at the dam. A valve is opened all the way.

+4 hrs
DETECTED

The dam's owner spots it and reverses it. About 497 litres a second more water than the minimum flows out.

Jun
THE VIDEO

Investigators find a three-minute video of the control panel with a pro-Russian group's watermark.

13 Aug
ATTRIBUTION

Norway's security service says pro-Russian hackers did it, aiming to create fear or unrest.

After
DENIAL

Russia's embassy calls the claim "unfounded and politically motivated."

Swipe the timeline sideways.

No one was hurt. The security service described it as less about causing damage than about showing what the hackers could do.

At work

Near anything remote-operated (valves, gates, pumps, breakers), ask one question: who can reach its control panel from outside the building? You don't need the answer. Someone should have it.

At home

Same logic for your garage door, cameras and thermostat. Use the maker's secure app, and turn off remote access you don't use.

Sources: Anadolu Agency; Reuters via Arctic Today; BleepingComputer. Attribution is Norway's; Russia denies it.

[04] // Which is it?

Crime. Spy. Or something else?

Crime wants money. Espionage wants secrets. Pre-positioning wants a switch. Three invented cases. Name each one.

CASE 1
A regional hospital's systems lock up overnight. A message on every screen demands payment in cryptocurrency.

Crime. The goal is money, and the attacker wants you to know. The Cyber Centre rates ransomware as the top cybercrime threat to Canada's critical infrastructure.

CASE 2
Over six months, someone quietly copies design files from a defence supplier. Production never stops.

Espionage. The goal is secrets, and the attacker hopes you never notice. Nothing is broken, because breaking things would end the access.

CASE 3
A water utility's IT network shows no data theft, no ransom and no damage. For a long time, a few admin accounts quietly sit one step away from the control systems.

Pre-positioning. Nothing is taken or broken, because the access itself is the prize. The goal is to be able to act later.

Score: 0 / 3

What the Five Eyes said

In February 2024, cyber agencies from the US, Canada, Australia, the UK and New Zealand warned that PRC state-sponsored actors were pre-positioning on IT networks, using tools already on those networks, so they could move on to operational technology and disrupt functions in a major crisis or conflict. The US agencies assessed this with high confidence, and said the targets and behaviour weren't consistent with traditional espionage. Canada's Cyber Centre co-signed it.

At work

When you report something odd, say what you saw, not what you think it is. Whether it's crime, spying or something else is for the specialists to work out.

At home

A router or camera that behaves strangely (slow for no reason, settings you didn't change) is worth a look, not a shrug.

Sources: CISA release (Feb 2024); Joint advisory AA24-038A; National Cyber Threat Assessment 2025-2026.

Same threat. Evidence closer each time.

At work

You don't have to be in a "critical" sector to be in the chain. Suppliers, contractors and shared services connect to the ones that are.

At home

Telecoms carry your calls, banking and alerts. Keep a paper list of key phone numbers and a battery bank for your phone.

[06] // Living off the land

Spot the intruder.

"Living off the land" means using the tools already on your systems, so there's no malware to catch. CISA called it a hallmark of Volt Typhoon. Here are three entries from an invented system log. One is the intruder.

The intruder is the 3 a.m. entry. It uses the same tool as the backup job, so antivirus sees nothing wrong. What gives it away is the time, the source and the purpose. That's why logging matters: the joint guidance stresses it, because you can't spot "different" if you never recorded "normal."

At work

An account logging in at 3 a.m., a setting nobody changed, a device you don't recognize: report it, even if it turns out to be nothing. Let the specialists decide.

At home

Look at your router's list of connected devices once. Anything you can't name is worth chasing down.

Sources: CISA et al., Identifying and Mitigating Living Off the Land Techniques; AA24-038A. Log entries are invented for training.

At work

[08] // The router in your house

Old gear is a doorway.

Volt Typhoon hid its tracks behind hundreds of hijacked home and small-office routers. Tap through a router's life.

At work

Branch-office routers, cameras and printers count too. Anything that has reached end-of-life and still sits on a network is a risk with a name on it.

At home

Check your router's age and support status tonight. Section 10 has the steps.

Sources: BleepingComputer (US Justice Department release); Infosecurity Magazine; Hackread; Cyber Centre ITSAP.80.019.

[09] // Accountability

The rules are coming.

Canada's critical-infrastructure cyber law passed in June 2026. Some of it applies now. The part that regulates operators doesn't yet.

2025
BILL C-8

Introduced in the 45th Parliament as An Act respecting cyber security.

26 Mar
HOUSE

The House of Commons passes it.

4 Jun
SENATE

The Senate adopts it.

Jun 2026
ROYAL ASSENT

It becomes law. The Telecommunications Act changes take effect right away.

Not yet
PART 2

The Critical Cyber Systems Protection Act needs a government order to come into force, and then designations of which operators it covers.

Swipe the timeline sideways.

What it means

The government says the Act will require designated operators in finance, telecommunications, energy and transportation to protect their critical cyber systems, and that it will be phased in. Nothing changes for you tomorrow. The point is that accountability is coming for the operators that run the systems in this brief, so there's no reason to wait for it to arrive.

Status as of early October 2026, from the government's release and law-firm analyses. Check LEGISinfo for the latest.

At work

If your employer is in one of those four sectors, ask whether anyone has started preparing. It's not your job to fix. It's worth asking.

At home

You don't need to do anything. It helps to know that the rules for the companies that keep your lights and phones on are getting stricter.

Sources: Public Safety Canada (16 Jun 2026); Digital Policy Alert; Lavery (21 Sep 2026); LEGISinfo. Parliament records Royal Assent on 15 June; the government's release is dated 16 June.

[10] // Before you go

One action. One conversation.

Do it tonight

Find your home router's make and model, and check whether it still gets security updates.

Good. That's one fewer old door for someone to walk through.

While you're in there (Cyber Centre guidance):

Done: 0 / 4

Take it home

  • Parents and grandparents: ask them to read the model number off their router, and help them check it.
  • Anyone with a router older than they can remember: replace it. It's a cheap fix for a large risk.
  • At work: ask who owns the list of routers, firewalls and VPNs, and who patches them.
  • If you see something that looks like tampering with a control system: stop, don't touch it, and report it now.

Where to report

  • At work: your supervisor and your security team first. Don't investigate on your own.
  • Organizations can report to the Cyber Centre through its online form. The Cyber Centre isn't law enforcement.
  • An imminent threat to life, or a crime: 911, local police or the RCMP.
  • Personal fraud: the CAFC at 1-888-495-8501.

If someone calls claiming to be the Cyber Centre, verify at 1-833-CYBER-88 before you share anything.
