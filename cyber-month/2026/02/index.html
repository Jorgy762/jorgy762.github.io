[02] // Incident // NATO exercise, Europe

The red team was on our side.

During a NATO exercise in an allied country, a red team from the NATO Strategic Communications Centre of Excellence in Riga went after the participating soldiers using nothing but social media. They had four weeks.

What they did

  • Built fake Facebook pages and closed groups that looked like they belonged to the exercise.
  • Used targeted advertising to pull soldiers into those pages.
  • Created profiles impersonating service members, some real, some invented.
  • Let the platform's own "suggested friends" feature point them to more targets.

One of the researchers summed up the method: every person has a button. For one it's money, for another an appealing date, for someone else it's family.

Four weeks. No AI. Just people, profiles and patience.

At work

Here's the part that should give you confidence: the report notes that some of the fake profiles and pages were reported by the very armed forces being targeted, and one warning got circulated. Reporting worked. It slowed the red team down.

At home

"Suggested friends" works on your family too. If your spouse or kids publicly follow your unit's pages or friend your section mates, they become a map to you.

Sources: NATO StratCom COE; Schneier on Security; LSM; researcher quote via Wired (2019).

[04] // Now add AI

The voice sounded right.

A cabinet secretary, cloned

In 2025, someone used AI to mimic US Secretary of State Marco Rubio's voice and writing style. They sent voice and text messages to three foreign ministers, a governor and a member of Congress, through a Signal account whose display name was made to look like his State Department address.

A campaign, not a one-off

The FBI says the wider campaign goes back to 2023 and targets officials, their families and their acquaintances. The pattern: move the target to Signal, Telegram or WhatsApp, then ask for authentication codes, ID documents, money or introductions.

At home in Canada

Canadians reported losing nearly $3 million to emergency and grandparent scams in 2024. A fraud examiner told CBC that 3 to 5 seconds of someone's voice is enough to clone it.

Your ear won't save you

The FBI warned in July 2026 that a cloned voice can sound nearly identical to the real person you know.

Why "read the code back" is the whole attack

That code is almost certainly a sign-in or account-recovery code for your Signal, WhatsApp or email. Read it back and you've handed over the account, and through it, your contacts. The FBI lists requests for authentication codes as a hallmark of the official-impersonation campaign. No real officer, bank or tech company will ever ask you to read a code back.

At work

Real requests survive a callback. If it's genuine, it'll still be genuine after you call the person on the number in the unit contact list.

At home

Teach the five moves at the dinner table. Kids and older parents are targeted with the same playbook, just a different costume.

[06] // Spot the fake

Trust it, or verify first?

Three rounds. All invented for training. Make the call.

ROUND 1 // VOICEMAIL TRANSCRIPT
"Hey, it's the CO. New number, my phone's done. Call me back on this one, not the office line. Something's come up about the weekend and I need you on it."

Verify first. New number, steering you away from the office line, and a vague urgent task: authority, urgency and a channel switch in three sentences. Call back on the number you already have, or through the unit. The voice itself proves nothing.

ROUND 2 // VIDEO CALL (WHAT YOU SEE AND HEAR)
A video call request from a brand-new account with your section commander's name. "Old account got locked." The face and voice look right. The picture is a little soft and the audio lags a beat.

"Pay office is moving everyone to a new system this week. I'll re-enrol you myself. Read me your SIN and the bank details you want your pay going to. Cut-off is today."

Verify first. A new account, a request for your SIN and banking details, and a same-day deadline. A real pay change doesn't arrive as a surprise video call. And looking right proves nothing: at the engineering group Arup, a finance employee joined a video call with what looked like the CFO and other colleagues, all deepfakes built from public video and audio, and made 15 transfers worth about US$25 million before checking with headquarters. Hang up. Contact the person on a number you already have, or go through your chain of command.

ROUND 3 // EMAIL ON YOUR WORK LAPTOP, 8:40 A.M.
From: IT Service Desk (the address is close to your company's, but not quite)
Subject: ACTION REQUIRED: your MFA expires today

To keep your access to email and files, re-enrol your authenticator before 5 p.m. Scan the code below with your phone's camera and sign in with your work account to confirm. Takes 30 seconds.
(Decorative pattern only. It doesn't scan.)

Verify first. The QR code is the trick. It's an image, so it's harder for email filters to catch, and unlike a link you can't hover over it to see where it goes. It also moves you off your managed work laptop and onto your personal phone, and the UK's National Cyber Security Centre notes that people are more likely to scan with a personal phone. The University of Toronto's security team warned its community about exactly this: emails with embedded QR codes that lead to fake sign-in pages built to steal credentials. The CAFC has warned that fraudsters insert QR codes into emails and texts to send people to fraudulent sites. Pages like that can be built to relay your password and your MFA code live, the adversary-in-the-middle trick from Brief 01. Don't scan. Open your company's portal by typing the address yourself, or ask IT through a channel you can check, and report the email.

Score: 0 / 3

Notice the answer was the same every time. That's the lesson: you can't tell by looking, so don't try. Verify.

Sources: CFO Dive on the Arup deepfake fraud; AI Incident Database, citing Hong Kong police; University of Toronto security on QR phishing; CAFC notice on QR code scams (Nov 2023); UK NCSC on QR codes; CBC on quishing.

[07] // Built for service members

The uniform changes the pitch.

Recruitment

The June 2026 Five Eyes warning: hostile intelligence services pose as recruiters and consultancies on professional and job sites. Your CV, employer history and specialist knowledge are what they want.

Sextortion

US Army investigators describe the pattern: a romance or explicit video chat, then a threat to send it to your chain of command or family unless you pay. They warn that follow-up demands can include sensitive military information or access to systems and facilities.

Your identity as bait

Army investigators have also seen service members' photos and identities stolen to run romance scams on others. Your photos in uniform can become someone else's costume.

Fake unit groups

The StratCom red team's best tool was a convincing "exercise" group. Fake unit, reunion and "fallen comrades" pages work the same way.

At work

Report a recruitment approach even if you said no. A declined approach still tells security staff who is being targeted and how.

At home

Tell your teens: if anyone threatens to share an image, stop replying, don't pay, keep the evidence, and tell an adult. For minors, Canada's tipline is Cybertip.ca.

Sources: Bitdefender (Five Eyes bulletin); US Army CID on sextortion; Army News Service; US Army CID on romance scams; NATO StratCom COE.

[08] // The verification habit

Their channel. Your check.

Call back

Hang up and call back on a number you already have, not one from the message. Caller ID can be spoofed.

Stay put

Never let them move you to a new app or number. A request to switch to Signal, Telegram or WhatsApp is a warning sign on its own.

Code word

Agree on a secret word or phrase with your family. The FBI recommends exactly this to beat cloned voices.

Generate a family code word

Three random words, created in your browser. Nothing is saved or sent. Use it or pick your own, then share it with family in person. Never text it or post it.

tap generate
At work

Sections can use the same idea: anything unusual by phone gets confirmed back through the orderly room or a known number. Make it normal, so nobody feels awkward checking.

At home

Practise once. Have a family member call with a fake "emergency" and ask for the word. The first time is the awkward time.

Sources: FBI IC3 PSA (Dec 2024), secret word guidance; channel-switch warning per FBI notices summarized by Scam Atlas.

[09] // If you're approached

Capture. Don't engage. Report.

Capture

  • Screenshots of the profile, the messages and any numbers, with dates and times.
  • The profile's web address, not just the display name, since names change.
  • What you did, if anything: replied, clicked, sent a photo, read back a code.

Don't engage

Don't confront them, play along or try to investigate. You'll tip them off and lose the evidence trail. Investigations belong to credentialed MP or police.

Report

Military-related: chain of command and unit security staff (USS / UISSO). Fraud: police and the CAFC at 1-888-495-8501. Sextortion: police; never pay. Scam texts: forward to 7726.

If you already clicked, paid or sent something: report anyway, and fast. Shame is what they count on.

Sources: CAFC; US Army CID; Get Cyber Safe (7726).
